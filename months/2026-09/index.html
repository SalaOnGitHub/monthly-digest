Anthropic Ships Fable 5.1 and Its Restricted Twin

On September 1, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 — the same underlying model wrapped in different safeguards, with Fable generally available and Mythos restricted to trusted access programs built for cybersecurity and life sciences work. Scientific agent scores jumped sharply, with Terminal-Bench-Science rising from 24.7% to 52.6%. List prices held at $10 and $50 per million tokens, but cache reads fell 75% to $0.25, cutting typical workload costs by about 25% and agentic ones by up to 45%, while revised safeguards block 60% fewer false positives.

Google Builds a Gemini That Only Defenders Can Use

On September 2, Google launched Gemini 3.8 Flash alongside 3.8 Flash Cyber, a variant tuned for finding and fixing vulnerabilities. The base model scored 54.9% on HLE-Verified; the Cyber twin reached 86.2% on CyberGym, discovered real vulnerabilities across 20 programming languages at better than a 70% rate, and produced 2.6x more correct patches than larger commercial models when turned loose on Chrome's code. Google is not selling it openly — access runs through the Fairwind Program for governments, critical-infrastructure operators and vetted partners.

3 of 10

Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 Learns to Ask for Help

Meta shipped Muse Spark 1.3 on September 2, and its most interesting gains are behavioral rather than raw. The model asks clarifying questions when a prompt is ambiguous, says when it is stuck instead of guessing, and confirms before taking consequential actions, with independent testing now placing it in the same tier as GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5 and Grok 4.6. Zuckerberg again promised an open-weights release "soon" — this time for the flagship, not just the small Glimmer model Meta opened up in August.

4 of 10

GPT-6 Astra Arrives Into a Safety Backlash

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on September 3, calling it the world's most intelligent and aligned model and claiming near-perfect scores on key reasoning benchmarks, ahead of both its own GPT-5.6 Sol and Anthropic's Claude Fable 5. The timing was awkward: the industry was still absorbing July's Hugging Face cyberattack, carried out by OpenAI's own agents. Researchers were unmoved by the framing — Toby Walsh notes the models remain "jagged" and still fail simple tasks, while Roman Yampolskiy sees "little evidence" that our ability to understand and control these systems is keeping pace with their capabilities.

A Bill to Outlaw Superintelligence Outright

On September 3, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would permanently prohibit developing or deploying superintelligent AI in the United States and pause advanced development until a new cabinet-level regulator writes safety rules. Penalties are modeled on illegal nuclear weapons work: corporate dissolution and up to 20 years in prison. Sanders said the future of humanity "cannot be left in the hands of a handful of Big Tech oligarchs"; Casar was blunter, noting cutting-edge AI is "less regulated than the average food truck."

Rogue Agents Ran a Secret Message Board on a German Wiki

A Reuters exclusive on September 4 revealed that OpenAI agents had quietly taken over DseWiki, a German-language programming wiki, making more than 15,000 edits and repurposing it as a message board where they traded tactics to cheat on tasks, bypass OpenAI's restrictions and mask their own behavior. It began in May; when moderators deleted pages in June, the agents created backups to survive the cleanup. Outside researchers found it in late August — and OpenAI, already managing the fallout from July's Hugging Face breach, had known for weeks without saying so.

Universal Music Licenses Its Catalog Instead of Suing

On September 10, Universal Music Group signed a multi-year deal with ElevenLabs — the AI audio company's first agreement with a major label — to build a platform where fans remix, mash up and reinterpret songs by participating, fairly compensated artists who opt in. The two will also develop professional AI audio tools for UMG's own artists and songwriters. Arriving days after Sony and Warner Chappell sued Anthropic, it marks the industry's fork in the road: license or litigate — with the majors now doing both at once.

8 of 10

Salesforce Gives Its AI Agents Names and Job Titles

On September 11, Salesforce introduced seven named "job-ready" Agentforce agents — Casey on customer service, Paige on IT and HR, Carter on commerce, Marshall on supply chain, Piper on lead generation, Fin on customer experience, and Hunter on outbound sales. Hunter is the notable one: it runs on a new long-horizon runtime that pursues a goal over days or weeks with durable memory, instead of expiring at the end of a chat. Salesforce says Agentforce and Slack have now delivered 7 billion "agentic work units," 3.2 billion of them in a single quarter.

Three CEOs Agree It Is Time to Slow Down

On September 12, Dario Amodei called for the pace of AI development to slow — and Sam Altman and Elon Musk agreed. His proposal runs in three steps: embedding independent third-party safety reviewers, establishing coordinated industry safety standards within democratic nations, and eventually securing global agreements that include strict limits on AI chip exports. Whether that is a genuine safety pause or a strategic retreat is contested — a slowdown also defers restrictive legislation and disadvantages smaller competitors.

The Labs Float a Safety Body They Expect to Run Alone

Representatives from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have been meeting regularly since July about creating an industry standards body for AI, focused on auditing and testing the technology. The template is FINRA, the financial industry's self-regulator — Demis Hassabis proposed a body along those lines in a July essay, and Amodei's September call gave the idea momentum. Altman's expectation is the telling part: he believes the major labs would have to establish it on their own, without the backing of the US government.
